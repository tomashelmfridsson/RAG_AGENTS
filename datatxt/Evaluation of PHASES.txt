Evaluation of PHASES Score for Predicting Rupture of Intracranial Aneurysms:
Signiﬁcance of Aneurysm Size
Samuel D. Pettersson1,2, Paulina Skrzypkowska1, Krzysztof Pietrzak1, Aleksander Och1, Kamil Siedlecki1,
Aleksandra Czapla-Iskrzycka3, Tomasz Klepinowski4, Thomas Fodor2, Jean Filo2, Jarosław Meyer-Szary5,
Justyna Fercho1, Fanny Sunesson1, Hanna K.L. Olofsson1, Shan Ali6, Tomasz Szmuda1, Grzegorz Miekisiak7
- BACKGROUND: Recent data have identified that certain
risk factors for rupture differ between small and larger
intracranial aneurysms (IAs). Such differing risk factors
make up 5 out of the 6 predictor variables used in the
PHASES score, which raises the question on whether IA
size has a significant effect on the score’s performance.
- METHODS: Patients who were diagnosed with an IA
incidentally or due to a subarachnoid hemorrhage between
2015 and 2023 were selected for potential inclusion. The
median IA size of the cohort was chosen as the cutoff point
to categorize small and large (6 mm). The PHASES score
was calculated for all patients, and a receiver operating
characteristic curve analysis was performed to evaluate
the classification accuracy of PHASES in predicting
rupture for small and large IAs.
- RESULTS: A total of 677 IAs were included. Among the
IAs, 400 (58.9%) presented as UIAs and 279 (41.0%) as
subarachnoid hemorrhage. The average PHASES score was
2.9 and 6.5 for small (n [ 322) and large (n [ 355) IAs,
respectively. The PHASES score performed significantly
lower for predicting rupture in smaller IAs (area under the
curve: 0.634) compared with the larger (area under the
curve: 0.741) (P [ 0.00083).
- CONCLUSIONS: PHASES was shown to underperform on
small IAs. The decision to treat small unruptured IAs
remains highly controversial, and the development of a
new score to estimate the annual rupture rate while ac-
counting for IA morphology is of great need. Our findings
can help encourage future researchers to develop such a
score.
INTRODUCTION
T
he decision to treat an unruptured intracranial aneurysm
(IA) can often be controversial. The natural history has
been well studied after the release of 2 major largescale
prospective observational studies: The International Study of
Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms (ISUIA)1,2 published in 1998
and the Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm Study of Japan (UCAS)3
in 2012. Despite having a stronger knowledge regarding the
natural history, or in other words, the annual rupture rate for
various types of IAs, there still remained no scoring system until
the mid-2010s, which could allow for a less opinion-based treat-
ment decision making process for unruptured IAs. In 2014,
PHASES became the ﬁrst major scoring system that sought to
alleviate this issue.4 PHASES is, in essence, a meta-analysis that
obtained the individual patient data of the 6 prospective unrup-
tured IA studies within the literature at its time to identify inde-
pendent predictor variables for rupture to develop a score that

either subgroup, the ﬁndings may suggest that predictor variable
modiﬁcations, or the development of a new scoring system built
on datasets of solely small or large IAs, may be of need.
METHODS
Data Extraction and Exclusion Criteria
This study was performed at the University Hospital of the Medical
University of Gdansk, Poland. All patients diagnosed with an IA
on diagnostic imaging between January 1, 2015, and January 1,
2023, were extracted retrospectively and evaluated for potential
inclusion. Exclusion criteria included (1) patients with one or more
fusiform, mycotic, or dissection IAs; (2) patients with other
vascular diseases such as arteriovenous malformation or moya-
moya disease, among others; (3) insufﬁcient diagnostic imaging
quality to evaluate IA size; and (4) patients with one or more IAs
previously treated at other hospitals.
Data Collection
The following patient characteristics are to be retrieved from
hospital records: age, sex, hypertension (blood pressure >140 mm
Hg systolic or on hypertensive medications), history of SAH, and
symptoms at admission. If a patient had multiple aneurysms, the
largest of these aneurysms, along with its location and maximum
diameter (the maximum distance of any 2 points on the aneu-
rysm), the imaging modality used for measuring the aneurysm
(digital subtraction angiography, magnetic resonance angiography
[MRA], or computed tomography angiography [CTA]), the
outcome (treatment or conservative management via scheduled
follow-up), whether treatment was initiated, and the type of
treatment (surgical clipping or endovascular) were collected. IAs
were subgrouped into unruptured and ruptured. Unruptured IAs
were those that presented incidentally due to imaging for another
reason or due to symptoms caused by IA mass effect. The ruptured
group were those that presented as an SAH. The diagnosis of SAH
at admission was conﬁrmed by computed tomography (CT) based

Statistical Analysis
The total cohort of aneurysms was stratiﬁed by size. To mitigate
bias, the median size of the aneurysm diameter (mm) was utilized
as the cutoff point to classify small and large aneurysms. The
cutoff point was later found to be 6 mm, which allowed a close to
even split (322:355). In this way, the receiver operating character-
istic curve analyses would have near to even statistical power for
both small and large aneurysms in terms of their sample sizes. All
collected continuous variables such as patient age and aneurysm
size are presented as means  standard deviations, while cate-
gorical variables are presented as percentages. T-test and Mann-
Whitney U test were used for continuous variables, and a chi-
square test was used for categorical variables. ROC curves are
empirical curves in the sensitivity and speciﬁcity space19: An area
under the curve (AUC) of 0.5 suggests no discrimination, while an
AUC of 1 suggests perfect discrimination. Statistical difference
between the small and large aneurysm ROC curves was tested
with de Long’s method.20 95% Conﬁdence intervals (CIs) of
sensitivity
and
speciﬁcity
were
computed
with
bootstrap
resampling and averaging methods.21 All statistical tests are 2
sided, and statistical signiﬁcance is deﬁned as P < 0.05. Finally,
to the best of our knowledge, at present 6 studies have
evaluated the performance of PHASES for predicting rupture.
These studies were pooled along with the present study in a
forest plot using random effects to obtain the overall AUC from
their ROC curve analyses. All statistical analyses and plots
generating were performed with R studio (version 1.4.1717), and
the following packages were utilized: readxl, pROC, ggplot2,
and metafor.
RESULTS
A total of 677 patients met the inclusion criteria and were involved
in the study. Among these patients, 492 (72.67%) were female and
the mean age was 58.39  14.1 years. Among the 677 aneurysms,
58.9 were unruptured and 41.1% were ruptured. Patient history of
a prior SAH was low (2.7%), and 53.5% were classiﬁed as having
hypertension. Regarding IAs, the average size was 7.0  4.5 mm
and 16.0% had multiple aneurysms (the distribution of IA size is
shown in Figure 1A). The most common IA location was at the
internal carotid artery (ICA) (30.6%), following the middle
cerebral artery (29.1%) and the anterior communicating artery
(27.2%). The most common image performed, which was later
used for measuring the aneurysm, was CTA (51.3%). Aneurysms
were treated in 63.8% of cases with surgical clipping being the
main approach utilized (51.6%). The average PHASES score for
the entire cohort was 4.8  3.2 points (Figure 1B shows the
PHASES score distribution stratiﬁed by UIA, SAH, and IA size).
All variables are stratiﬁed by aneurysm size in Table 2.
The ROC curve analysis reported a signiﬁcantly lower classiﬁ-
cation accuracy by the PHASES score for small IAs (AUC: 0.634)
compared with larger ones (AUC: 0.741) (P < 0.001). The AUC for
the total cohort was 0.62 (95% CI 0.560e0.680) (Figure 2.).

DISCUSSION
The present study identiﬁed that the PHASES score signiﬁcantly
underperforms on smaller (<6 mm) aneurysms when predicting
rupture. This ﬁnding is indeed of great interest to cerebrovascular
specialist given that small aneurysms have remain poorly under-
stood regarding their natural history compared to their larger
counterparts. This is due to the conﬂicting results reported by the
UCAS and ISUIA trials. As mentioned, both trials have been the
major sources used to base decision-making for UIAs. Both pro-
spectively assessed the annual rupture rates of UIAs of various
sizes and found similar results. However, for small aneurysms the
2 reported conﬂicting results. ISUIA identiﬁed a risk of hemor-
rhage for aneurysms <7 mm to be 0% over 5 years for group 1
patients (single unruptured lesion) in the anterior circulation and
1.5% for group 2 patients (prior subarachnoid hemorrhage [SAH]
from another aneurysm). For posterior circulation lesions, the
rates were 2.5% over 5 years for group 1 patients and 3.4% for
group 2 patients.2 UCAS had a much higher percent of smaller
aneurysms with an overall annual risk of hemorrhage of 0.36%
for lesions 34 mm.3 The conﬂicting results between the two
trails has made the decision-making process for smaller unrup-
tured IAs difﬁcult and controversial. As a result, revaluating
common and establishing new predictor variables for rupture have
been a growing interest in the literature especially for small an-
eurysms.12,22-35 On a side note, it is shown in the literature that the

Past Modifications to PHASES and New Scores
Modiﬁcations in the form of adding new predictor variable to
PHASES has already been attempted.18 In 2021, Juvela addressed
the controversy on why smoking was not included in PHASES
which has been long regarded as an important factor for IA
rupture given the ﬁndings from the UCAS and ISUIA trails. As a
result, Juvela investigated whether the inclusion of the variable,
smoking, would enhance the classiﬁcation accuracy of PHASES
when used to predict rupture. As suspected, the modiﬁed score
with smoking included reported an improved AUC (0.755, 95%
CI 0.657e0.853) compared to PHASES (0.674, 95% CI 0.558e
0.790) when applied onto a prospective dataset of 142 Finnish
patients (note: this dataset was 1 of the 6 prospective datasets
used in the development of PHASES). However, there are other
studies in the recent years which took an additional step in
developing their own score with a focus on morphology related
predictors.14,16,17,34 Unlike the study by Juvela, these scores were
derived
from
retrospective
datasets
and
thus
should
be
interpreted with caution. We know that aneurysm morphology
not only are shared predictors for both small and large
aneurysms but have consistently shown to outperform clinical
predictors in the literature.12,22-35 Thus, these pilot studies which
have developed their scores that allocate signiﬁcant weight to
morphology, are also reporting substantially improved perfor-
mance compared with PHASES. Liu et al in 2021 was the ﬁrst of its
class to develop and validate its model, which reported a signiﬁ-
cantly improved AUC (0.837 [95% CI 0.780e0.894]) compared
with PHASES (0.657, [95% CI 0.585e0.729]) on their dataset of
658 aneurysms (P < 0.001).14 The same and following year yielded
other similar scores, which focused on solely small aneurysms16,34
or utilizing machine learning,17 all which involved mainly
morphologic
predictors.
Due
to
their
study
design
being
retrospective in nature, it is needed to validate their results with
prospective data before clinical implementation.

Limitation
The present study contains several limitations. First, only a Polish
population (classiﬁed as North American, European [other than
Finnish] in PHASES) was involved, which warrants the need for
future studies to validate our ﬁndings with a Japanese and Finnish
population. However, the meta-analysis does provide some insight
that our ﬁndings are likely generalizable given that the total cohort
reported similar AUC results compared with studies involving
Finnish and other non-Finnish European populations. Finally,
aneurysm measurement was performed using a broad range of
imaging modalities. It is likely that our measurements for the
maximum aneurysm diameter may be affected given the variations
in image resolution among CTA, DSA, and MRA. Furthermore, IA
measurement was performed by several neurosurgeons spanning a
wide range in years of clinical experience (3e20 years). However, it
has been shown that a neurosurgeon’s years of experience does
have a signiﬁcant effect on the precision on aneurysm measure-
ments on diagnostic imaging.38
CONCLUSION
The present study is the ﬁrst to investigate and report that aneu-
rysm size has a signiﬁcant effect on PHASES score performance
for predicting rupture. At present, the treatment decision making
for small aneurysms has several pitfalls, making the process
difﬁcult and often controversial.39 The ﬁndings from this study
suggest that in addition to the discrepancies seen in the natural
history for small unruptured IAs from the UCAS and ISUIA
trials, PHASES is likely not aiding the controversial treatment
decision making process for IAs at the smaller end of the
spectrum as initially hoped. However, we strongly encourage
future prospective studies to validate and build off of our ﬁndings.
