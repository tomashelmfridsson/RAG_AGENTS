Association of elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio with
increased intracranial aneurysm stability scores and aneurysm
growth
Baorui Zhang, MD,a,1 Yongkai Qin, MS,b,1 Shangfeng Zhao, MD,a
Wei Wang, MD,a Lang Liu, MS,b Siyuan Dong, MD,b Yan Miao, MD,c
Songfeng Zhao, MD,b Zhongxue Wu, MD,d Jun Kang, PhD,a and Aihua Liu, PhDd
Background and purpose: Inﬂammation involves in the progression of intracranial
aneurysms (IAs). However, whether the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as
an inﬂammatory marker links to IAs stability is unidentiﬁed. This study was per-
formed to assess the association of the NLR with IAs stability. Methods: We retro-
spectively reviewed the medical records of patients diagnosed with unruptured IAs
from January 2014 to June 2018. According to the quartiles of the NLR, patients
with unruptured IAs were categorized into four groups. We evaluated the associa-
tion between the NLR and IAs stability scores and IAs growth. Multiple logistic
regression models were used in the analysis. Results: A signiﬁcant dose-response
association was found between the NLR with IAs stability scores and IAs growth.
After adjustment for potential confounders, an elevated NLR (fourth quartile) was
associated with increased PHASES score (>5) (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 2.007; 95%
conﬁdence interval [CI], 1.3612.960; p<0.001 [p for trend <0.001]), increased
ELAPSS score (>15) (adjusted OR, 1.581; 95% CI, 1.0742.328; p=0.020 [p for trend
=0.001]), increased JAPAN 3-year rupture risk score (>5) (adjusted OR, 1.512; 95%
CI, 1.0332.215; p=0.034 [p for trend <0.001]), and IAs growth (adjusted OR,
16.759; 95% CI, 3.02292.928; p=0.001 [p for trend <0.001]). Conclusion: An elevated
NLR was associated with increased IAs stability scores and IAs growth. The associ-
ation between NLR and IAs stability need further investigate.

Introduction
Intracranial aneurysms (IAs) affect approximately 2%
to 4% of the adult population.13 If the IAs ruptures,
the mortality is as high as 30-40%.4,5 Therefore, screen-
ing unstable IAs for appropriate intervention is crucial
to reduce the death risk of IAs. Several growth and
rupture
risk
scores
for
predicting
the
stability
of
unruptured IAs have been established, including the
PHASES score,6 ELAPSS score,2 and JAPAN 3-year
rupture risk score.7 Although the biological factors
contributing
to
aneurysm
growth
remain
unclear,
aneurysm growth is pathologically associated with
destruction of the inner elastic layer secondary to
impairment of vascular smooth muscle cells, which
further leads to disordered aneurysm wall collagen
synthesis and extracellular matrix remodeling.8,9

Long-term clinical observations have revealed that sev-
eral leukocytes, including neutrophils, lymphocytes, and
monocytes/macrophages,
inﬁltrate
the
adventitia
of
aneurysms and have potential roles in the pathophysiol-
ogy of IAs.10 The presence of neutrophil-derived enzymes
in the aneurysm wall is associated with a signiﬁcant
increase in the risk of rupture.11 Recent studies have
shown that neutrophil extracellular traps promote the
development of IAs rupture.12 Lymphocytes have been
shown to play an important role in inﬂammatory diseases
associated with aneurysms, such as atherosclerosis.13,14
Lymphocytes can promote extracellular matrix remodel-
ing and phenotypic regulation of vascular smooth muscle
cells, thereby affecting the development of IAs.15 In addi-
tion, the latest research shows that subsets of circulating
leukocytes, such as neutrophils and lymphocytes, are
associated with the development of abdominal aortic
aneurysms.16
The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is a measure
of the proportion of systemic neutrophils and lympho-
cytes, and this ratio has been considered a reliable com-
posite
marker
and
dynamic
index
of
systemic
inﬂammation.17,18 However, few data on the relationship
between the NLR and aneurysm stability are available. In
addition, NLR is convenient in practical application
because neutrophil and lymphocyte counts are routinely
measured during laboratory testing. NLR can provide rel-
atively cheap diagnosis for clinical treatment.
This study was performed to investigate the relation-
ship of the NLR with the stability and growth of unrup-
tured IAs.
Methods
Study design and patients
We identiﬁed unruptured IAs patients, older than
18 years, were consecutively admitted to Beijing Tiantan
Hospital, a large comprehensive stroke center located in
Beijing, China, from January 2014 to June 2018. The diag-
nosis of unruptured IAs was conﬁrmed by CTA, MRA,
and/or DSA. We excluded cerebral AVM, AVF, or moya-
moya disease related aneurysms, as well as dissecting,
fusiform, traumatic, and mycotic aneurysms, and patients
with systematic inﬂammatory, infectious diseases, or can-
cer. Ethics approval was obtained from the hospital
review committee. All participants provided informed
consent. This study complies with the principles of the
Declaration of Helsinki.
Data collection
We reviewed the electronic medical records and inte-
grated the demographic, clinical, and laboratory data into
our neurovascular database. We collected the patients’
demographics and medical history, including subarach-
noid
hemorrhage,
ischemic
stroke,
hypertension,
hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, heart disease, smoking
status, and drinking status. The laboratory data were col-
lected after patient admission. Blood samples were tested
as follows: First, venous blood samples were collected by
venipuncture and placed in K2-EDTA anticoagulation
tubes. Second, blood samples were analyzed within 6 h
by an autoanalyzer (Mindray Hematology Analyzer BC-
6900 series; Mindray Corporation, Shenzhen, China) in
the hospital. The reference ranges of the assay were as fol-
lows: white blood cells, 4.0-10.0 109/L; neutrophils, 2.0-
7.5 109/L; and lymphocytes, 0.9-5.2 109/L. Multiple blood
tests were performed during the imaging follow-up, and
the mean of the blood sample analysis results were used
for data assessment.
Image analysis and aneurysm stability evaluation
All images were read by two readers who had 4 and
5 years of experience in vascular neuroimaging, respec-
tively, and who were blinded to each other’s interpreta-
tions and the patients’ clinical information. Before the
image evaluation, we conducted Kappa test (kappa >0.8),
which indicated that the results of aneurysm characteris-
tics judged by the two readers were high consistency. In
the process of image evaluation, discordances over the
evaluation results between the two readers were resolved
through discussion, with the involvement of senior radiol-
ogist with 10 years of experience when necessary. Discor-
dances were deﬁned as: (1) For categorical variables,
inconsistent discrimination of the shape, and aneurysmal
location; and (2) for the continuous variables, such as
aneurysm size, neck width, maximum height, maximum
width, parent vessel diameter, the measurement differ-
ence between the two readers is greater than 1 mm.
Aneurysm stability was evaluated by the PHASES
score,1,6 ELAPSS score,2 and JAPAN 3- year rupture risk
score.7 The PHASES score for each aneurysm was calcu-
lated by 6 factors: population (0, North American, Euro-
pean (other than Finnish); 3, Japanese; 5, Finnish),
hypertension (0, no; 1, yes), age (0, <70 years; 1, 70
years), earlier SAH from another aneurysm (0, no; 1, yes),
size of aneurysm (0, <7.0 mm; 3, 7.09.9 mm; 6,
10.019.9 mm; 10, 20.0 mm), aneurysm site (0, internal
carotid artery; 2, middle cerebral artery; 4, anterior cere-
bral arteries, posterior communicating artery, and poste-
rior circulation).19 ELAPSS score is based on 6 factors:
earlier subarachnoid hemorrhage(no; yes), location of the
aneurysm(ICA / ACA / ACOM; MCA; PCOM / Poste-
rior circulation), age(>60 years (per 5 years)), population,
size of the aneurysm(1.0-2.9; 3.0-4.9; 5.0-6.9; 7.0-9.9; 10
(mm)), and shape of the aneurysm(regular; irregular).20
JAPAN 3-year rupture risk score has six predictors as fol-
lows: age, sex, hypertension, aneurysmal size, aneurysmal
location, and presence of daughter sac.7 According to the
risk of aneurysm rupture or growth within 5 or 3 years,
we classiﬁed unstable aneurysms as those with a PHASES

score of >5, ELAPSS score of >15, and JAPAN 3-year rup-
ture risk score of >4. We used PHASES, ELAPSS, and
JAPAN 3-year rupture risk score to predict aneurysm
growth because their scoring items and structure are simi-

PHASES scores can predict the growth of aneurysms.1
Follow-up imaging and aneurysm growth
Follow-up imaging was performed at 6 and 12 months
and then annually for 48 months after diagnosis. Aneu-
rysm growth was deﬁned according to the following three
criteria: growth of 1.0 mm in at least one direction as
shown by identical or different imaging modalities,
growth of 0.5 mm in two directions as shown by identi-
cal imaging modalities; or an indisputable change in
aneurysm shape (i.e., change from regular shape to irreg-
ular shape).1 The imaging follow-up time for all aneur-
ysms was at least 6 months.
Statistical analysis
The baseline variables were compared between the
groups using the x2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categori-
cal variables and one-way analysis of variance or the
KruskalWallis test for continuous variables with a nor-
mal or skewed distribution, respectively. Continuous var-
iables are expressed as mean § standard deviation or
median with interquartile range. Categorical data are pre-
sented as proportions.
We took the fourth quintile with the lowest incidence of
outcomes as a reference and performed logistic regression
to explore the associations of the NLR with the aneurysm
stability score and aneurysm growth. Adjusted odds
ratios (ORs) and their 95% conﬁdence intervals (CIs) were
calculated. We used two models. In the ﬁrst model, no
variable adjustment was performed. In the second model,
we included all potential confounding variables.
In addition, we used restricted cubic splines to examine
the shape of the association of the NLR with the aneurysm
stability score and aneurysm growth, with four knots (at
the 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles); the reference
point for the NLR was NLR=2, and the OR was adjusted
for all potential covariates (Model 2).
Additionally, we also used the C statistic, net reclassiﬁ-
cation index (NRI), and integrated discrimination index
(IDI) to evaluate the incremental predictive value of the
NLR beyond the PHASES score, ELAPSS score, JAPAN 3-
year rupture risk score, conventional model, and aneu-
rysm growth. With reference to previous studies, the risk
category for aneurysm growth was set at <5%, 5% to
<10%, and >10%.1,2 Categorical net reclassiﬁcation was
evaluated by cross-tabulating the participants into three
categories of <5%, 5% to <10%, and >10% before and
after addition of the NLR to the predictive model.
All statistical analyses were performed using R soft-
ware
version

(R

for

<0.05 was considered statistically signiﬁcant.

Discussion
Systemic inﬂammation may contribute the local inﬂam-
mation reaction of IAs. Here, we show for the ﬁrst time
that aneurysms in the highest NLR quartile, compared
with those in the lowest quartile, had a 2.007-fold higher
risk of aneurysm instability as assessed by the PHASES

Aneurysm growth is related to pathological vascular
remodeling caused by the recruitment and inﬁltration of
inﬂammatory cells.21 The recruitment and inﬁltration of
inﬂammatory cells further increases cytokine
levels,
matrix degradation, collagenase and protease concentra-
tions, and apoptosis, leading to a decrease in the numbers
of smooth muscle cells and weakening of the aneurysm
structure; these changes ultimately result in aneurysm
growth.8,9 Among these phenomena, the recruitment and
inﬁltration of inﬂammatory cells (such as monocytes,
macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes) to the vessel
wall is the key stage of aneurysm growth.10Neutrophils
are the ﬁrst-responding inﬂammatory cells and migrate to
the site of injury in the vascular wall. Studies of human IA
specimens have shown that an elevated concentration of
plasma neutrophil elastase in patients with IAs is associ-
ated with vascular wall degeneration.22,23 In addition, the
presence of neutrophilic enzymes in aneurysm walls is
associated with a signiﬁcantly increased risk of rupture,
suggesting that neutrophils may play a causal role in the
progression from aneurysm growth to rupture.11 A recent
study also showed that neutrophilic extracellular traps
promote the development of IA rupture in animal aneu-
rysm models.12 Furthermore, neutrophils can secrete cyto-
kines, such as tumor necrosis factor-a, CXC chemokine,
macrophage inﬂammatory protein-1a, and interleukin-
1b, and further promote the recruitment, migration, acti-
vation, and differentiation of inﬂammatory cells.24 Our
ﬁnding that higher numbers of circulating neutrophils are
associated with aneurysm growth supports the notion
that neutrophils promote the inﬂammatory progression of
aneurysms from an epidemiological perspective.

Lymphocytes are another major inﬂammatory cell
found in aneurysms. T-cell inﬁltrates have also been
found in human aneurysms, indicating that the immune
system is widely involved in aneurysm growth.25 In an
animal model of elastase-induced aneurysms, T lympho-
cytes were observed to inﬁltrate the aneurysm wall.26 T
cells are the dominant cells in the aneurysm wall and are
involved in the production of proinﬂammatory cytokines
such as tumor necrosis factor-a, interferon-g, and inter-
leukin-6.10 Other studies have shown that lymphocyte-
deﬁcient Rag1 animals had a lower rate of aneurysm for-
mation and rupture.15 In the present study, lymphocytes
were not associated with aneurysm growth; however, this
does not negate the role of lymphocytes in aneurysm pro-
gression. A previous study showed that inﬁltration of
lymphocytes into the arterial wall occurs in the early
stages of aneurysm formation.27 In addition, that study
used peripheral circulating lymphocyte counts, and no
further lymphocyte typing was performed; this may
contribute to the difference between previous results and
our results.
The NLR is the ratio of neutrophils to lymphocytes
within the body and is a comprehensive index of nonspe-
ciﬁc immunity and speciﬁc immunity.18,2830 According
to the pathological mechanism of aneurysm progression,
the NLR seems to be related to the stability of aneurysm
growth. Few epidemiological studies have focused on
changes in the NLR in cerebral aneurysms. The present
study is the ﬁrst to show an association between the NLR
and the stability of unruptured aneurysms. A recent study
showed that the formation of abdominal aortic aneurysms
is related to lymphocytes and neutrophils.16 However, the
microenvironments
differ
between
abdominal
aortic
aneurysms and IAs. Other studies also showed that the
NLR is related to the severity of coronary artery disease.16
The NLR usually reﬂects the body’s chronic inﬂamma-
tory state.31,32 In this study, the NLR was related to aneu-

This is the ﬁrst systematic study to report the associa-
tion of the NLR with the stability and growth of unrup-
tured aneurysms based on routine clinical data. However,
we also acknowledge several limitations. First, the imag-
ing follow-up of unruptured aneurysms was performed
in selected patients with a lower risk of aneurysm growth.
This may have resulted in underestimation of the overall
risk of growth of unruptured aneurysms. Second, the

Conclusions
An elevated NLR was associated with an increased
aneurysm stability score and aneurysm growth. This may
help physicians to evaluate the stability and growth of
unruptured IAs in clinical practice.
