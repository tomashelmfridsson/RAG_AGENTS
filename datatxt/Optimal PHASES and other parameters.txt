Optimal PHASES Scoring for Risk Stratiﬁcation of Surgically Treated Unruptured
Aneurysms

- OBJECTIVE: The PHASES (Population, Hypertension, Age,
Size, Earlier subarachnoid hemorrhage, Site) score was
developed to facilitate risk stratification for management of
unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs). This study aimed
to identify the optimal PHASES score cutoff for predicting
neurologic outcomes in patients with surgically treated
aneurysms.
- METHODS: All
patients
who
underwent
micro-
neurosurgical treatment for UIA at a large quaternary
center from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2020, were
retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria included a
modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of £2 at admission. The
primary outcome was 1-year mRS score, with a “poor”
neurologic outcome defined as an mRS score >2.
- RESULTS: In total, 375 patients were included in the
analysis. The mean (SD) PHASES score for the entire study
population was 4.47 (2.67). Of 375 patients, 116 (31%) had a
PHASES score ‡6, which was found to maximize prediction
of poor neurologic outcome. Patients with PHASES scores
‡6 had significantly higher rates of poor neurologic
outcome than patients with PHASES scores <6 at discharge
(58 [50%] vs. 90 [35%], P [ 0.005) and follow-up (20 [17%]
vs. 18 [6.9%], P [ 0.002). After adjusting for age, Charlson
Comorbidity
Index
score,
nonsaccular
aneurysm,
and aneurysm size, PHASES score ‡6 remained a signifi-
cant predictor of poor neurologic outcome at follow-up
(odds ratio, 2.75; 95% confidence interval, 1.42e5.36, P [
0.003).
- CONCLUSIONS: In this retrospective analysis, a PHASES
score ‡6 was associated with significantly greater pro-
portions of poor outcome, suggesting that awareness of
this threshold in PHASES scoring could be useful in risk
stratification and UIA management.

These dangers suggest the value of prophylactically treating
UIAs. However, both endovascular and open surgical treatments
pose

Because

may

physicians must thoughtfully weigh the risk of rupture against
the risks associated with treatment to ensure optimal patient
outcomes. Accurately determining the risk of aneurysm rupture
plays a crucial role in deciding between observation and
treatment.

Greving et al.8 developed a practical risk-scoring system called the
PHASES (Population, Hypertension, Age, Size, Earlier subarach-
noid hemorrhage, Site) score. Released in 2014, the PHASES score
uses routinely assessed patient and aneurysm characteristics to
determine the 5-year risk of aneurysm rupture. Because of its use
in decision-making regarding the clinical treatment of unruptured
aneurysms,9 the PHASES score has been criticized for not taking
into
account
the
risks
associated
with
surgical
aneurysm
treatment.10,11 In an effort to elucidate the association between
the PHASES score and treatment risk, we sought to evaluate
whether the PHASES score is predictive of neurologic outcome
in patients with surgically treated UIAs.
METHODS
All patients who underwent surgical treatment for a UIA between
January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2020, at a single quaternary
center (Barrow Neurological Institute, St. Joseph’s Hospital and
Medical Center, Phoenix, Arizona) were identiﬁed in a retro-
spective research database. Inclusion criteria were the availability
of treatment data, adequate follow-up, and a modiﬁed Rankin
Scale (mRS) score of 2 at admission. Adequate follow-up was
deﬁned as at least 1 in-ofﬁce visit more than 7 days from the index
hospitalization related to the aneurysm operation. Patients were
grouped into 2 cohorts based on PHASES scoring (6 vs. <6).8
PHASES scores were calculated for all patients; for patients with
multiple aneurysms, the highest PHASES score was used. The
primary outcome was 1-year mRS score, with a poor neurologic
outcome deﬁned as an mRS score of >2.
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board for
Human Research at St. Joseph’s Hospital and Medical Center in
Phoenix and complied with the Health Insurance Portability and
Accountability Act. The need for patient consent was waived by the
institutional review board because of the retrospective nature of
the study. Electronic medical records were analyzed for de-
mographic information (age, sex, and comorbidities), American
Society of Anesthesiologists classiﬁcation, Charlson Comorbidity
Index (CCI) score, and life expectancy as determined by CCI score.
Aneurysm characteristics obtained from computed tomography or
angiogram ﬁndings were collected to assess for aneurysm
maximum
diameter,
neck
diameter,
height
perpendicular,
maximum height, calciﬁcation, and type; critical perforating or
branch vessels; intraluminal thrombosis; and size and aspect
ratio.
Statistical analyses included data aggregation, exploratory
analysis, and multivariate analysis using R, version 4.0.1 (R
Foundation for Statistical Computing). Optimal cutoff points for
continuous variables were determined using Euclidean distance
analysis onto mRS score >2 at ﬁnal follow-up. Univariate analysis
was used to initially determine variable association with outcome.
All variables with a P value <0.2 on univariate analysis were
included in a multivariable logistic regression analysis. De-
mographic and clinical characteristics of patients were analyzed
using an independent 2-sample t-test for interval variables and a
c2 test for categorical variables. Variables that compared medians
were calculated using Mood’s median test. Signiﬁcance was
deﬁned as P < 0.05. Results are reported as mean (SD), median
(interquartile range [IQR]), or number (percentage).
RESULTS
In total, 375 patients were included in the analysis; 282 (75%) were
women, and the mean (SD) age was 58 (12) years. Eighty-four
patients (22%) had multiple aneurysms treated. The mean (SD)
PHASES score for the entire study population was 4.47 (2.67). The
mean (SD) length of follow-up was 369 (637) days. The optimal
cutoff analysis determined that a PHASES score of 6 maximized
prediction of poor neurologic outcome. Of 375 patients, 116 (31%)
had a PHASES score 6 and 259 (69%) had a PHASES score <6.
Patients with PHASES scores 6 were signiﬁcantly older than
patients with PHASES scores <6 (63 [11] vs. 56 [11] years, P <
0.001) and were signiﬁcantly more likely to have hypertension (87
[75%] vs. 140 [54%], P < 0.001) and a shorter life expectancy (P <
0.001; Table 1). A greater proportion of patients with PHASES
scores 6 had experienced previous SAH (19 [16%] vs. 20
[7.7%], P ¼ 0.01), as well as preoperative anticoagulant and
antiplatelet use (44 [38%] vs. 71 [27%], P ¼ 0.04) compared
with patients with PHASES scores <6. mRS score at admission,
sex,
drug
and
alcohol
use,
hypercholesterolemia,
hyperlipidemia, and diabetes were not signiﬁcantly different
between the 2 cohorts. Patients with PHASES scores 6 had
signiﬁcantly higher median (IQR) mRS score at last follow-up (1
[1e2] vs. 1 [1e1]; P ¼ 0.004), as well as higher rates of poor
neurologic outcome (i.e., mRS score >2) at discharge (58 [50%]
vs. 90 [35%], P ¼ 0.005) and follow-up (20 [17%] vs. 18 [6.9%],
P ¼ 0.002; Table 2). After adjusting for optimized cutoffs for age,
CCI, nonsaccular aneurysms, and aneurysm size, PHASES score
6 remained a signiﬁcant predictor of poor neurologic outcome
at follow-up (odds ratio, 2.75; 95% conﬁdence interval, 1.42e
5.36, P ¼ 0.003; Table 3). In fact, PHASES score 6 was a stronger
predictor
of
the
aforementioned
confounders.
Overall,
the
PHASES score 6 threshold alone had a speciﬁcity of 71.5%,
sensitivity of 52.6%, Youden Index of 0.241, accuracy of 69.6%,
and area under the curve of 0.591.
DISCUSSION
With advanced diagnostic imaging has come an increased number
of incidentally discovered aneurysms, and additional tools are
needed to guide management and predict treatment outcomes for
these aneurysms. Here, we provide evidence that the PHASES
score could potentially serve as a valuable tool to not only assess
the risk of UIA rupture but also predict treatment outcomes. The
optimal cutoff analysis determined that a PHASES score of 6
maximized prediction of neurologic outcome (Figure 1). In our
study, the percentage of patients with unfavorable neurologic
outcomes after a mean follow-up period of 1 year increased
from 7% to 17% and thus increased by a factor of approximately
2.5 in patients with PHASES scores 6. Moreover, PHASES scores
6 are associated with an almost three fold increase in the odds of
having a poor neurologic outcome after a mean follow-up period
of 1 year. Therefore the data in this study showed that a signiﬁ-
cantly greater proportion of patients with PHASES scores 6 have
a poor neurologic outcome after undergoing surgical treatment for
a UIA. Similarly, previous publications have demonstrated that
PHASES scores were an independent predictor of poor functional
outcomes in patients after aneurysmal SAH.12 However, because of
this study’s focus on patients receiving prophylactic care for UIAs,

Some studies have examined other parameters in an attempt to
reﬁne the predicted risk of aneurysm rupture. Patient-related risk
factors such as tobacco use,20,21 female sex,22 family history of
SAH or aneurysm,2 collagenopathies,23 aneurysm shape,24 and
changes in aneurysm size25 are not currently accounted for in
the PHASES score. With the development of new imaging
technologies and analytic techniques, aneurysm-related risk fac-
tors for rupture have also been more precisely deﬁned. Liu et al.26
developed a nomogram-based prediction tool that relied on the
extraction of quantitative imaging features from radiographic
images for aneurysm rupture risk stratiﬁcation. Their quantitative
assessment of aneurysm irregularity—in particular, surface-to-
volume
ratio
and
aneurysm

Zwarzany et al.27 showed that aneurysm wall enhancement on
high-resolution vessel wall magnetic resonance imaging, which
is believed to suggest inﬂammatory changes in the aneurysm wall,
correlated with known markers of aneurysm instability. Interest-
ingly, Zwarzany et al.27 also found that aneurysms with a larger
enhancement area had signiﬁcantly higher PHASES scores. Ou

Although other arguably superior scoring systems are being
increasingly adopted, there is growing evidence questioning the
utility of the PHASES score to accurately predict aneurysm rupture
risk. With an approximate accuracy of 70% for correctly predicting
outcomes, our data support that the PHASES score may, in fact,
serve as a valuable tool to predict treatment outcomes, rather than
risk of rupture.
Limitations
The main limitation of this study is its retrospective nature and its
lack of long-term follow-up data. In general, neither PHASES nor
any other scoring system can make completely accurate assump-
tions and predictions of patient outcomes. Because the patients in
our cohort were treated by highly experienced neurosurgeons at a
specialized, large-volume quaternary center, the outcome data
might not be directly comparable with that for other centers. Only
the PHASES scoring system was analyzed in this study, which
limits direct comparison with other scoring systems. The PHASES
threshold results were signiﬁcant in the univariate and multivar-
iate models onto patient outcomes; however, evaluation of the
score’s performance must also consider the strength of predict-
ability, which was limited by a relatively low sensitivity when
considered alone.
CONCLUSION
Diagnostic imaging has revealed an increasing number of UIAs,
but the decision to treat incidentally discovered aneurysms is
challenging. This study demonstrated that PHASES scores 6 are
associated with an approximately threefold increase in the per-
centage of patients who had a poor neurologic outcome (i.e., mRS
score >2) after undergoing surgical treatment for unruptured
aneurysms. These ﬁndings suggest that PHASES scoring at this
threshold may be useful in predicting the treatment outcome for
the patient and thus potentially serve as a valuable tool for risk
stratiﬁcation and management for UIAs.
