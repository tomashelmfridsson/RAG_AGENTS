- OBJECTIVE: There are few randomized data comparing
clipping and coiling for middle cerebral artery (MCA)
aneurysms. We analyzed results from patients with MCA
aneurysms enrolled in the CURES (Collaborative UnRup-
tured Endovascular vs. Surgery) and ISAT-2 (International
Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial II) randomized trials.
- METHODS: Both trials are investigator-led parallel-
group 1:1 randomized studies. CURES includes patients
with 3-mm to 25-mm unruptured intracranial aneurysms
(UIAs), and ISAT-2 includes patients with ruptured aneu-
rysms (RA) for whom uncertainty remains after ISAT. The
primary outcome measure of CURES is treatment failure: 1)
failure to treat the aneurysm, 2) intracranial hemorrhage
during follow-up, or 3) residual aneurysm at 1 year. The
primary outcome of ISAT-2 is death or dependency
(modified Rankin Scale score >2) at 1 year. One-year
angiographic outcomes are systematically recorded.
- RESULTS: There were 100 unruptured and 71 ruptured
MCA aneurysms. In CURES, 90 patients with UIA have been
treated and 10 await treatment. Surgical and endovascular
management of unruptured MCA aneurysms led to treat-
ment failure in 3/42 (7%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.02e
0.19) for clipping and 13/48 (27%; 95% CI, 0.17e0.41) for
coiling (P [ 0.025). All 71 patients with RA have been
treated. In ISAT-2, patients with ruptured MCA aneurysms
managed surgically had died or were dependent (modified
Rankin Scale score >2) in 7/38 (18%; 95% CI, 0.09e0.33)
cases, and 8/33 (24%; 95% CI, 0.13e0.41) for endovascular.

INTRODUCTION
A
neurysms located on the middle cerebral artery (MCA) are
considered by many surgeons to represent a distinct
subgroup of intracranial aneurysms for which clipping
may still be the best management option. Most MCA aneurysms
are accessible, proximal control can readily be secured, and clip
application can typically proceed without requiring the dissection
of perforating arteries. In comparison, certain anatomic features
of MCA aneurysms such as a wide neck, often including a branch
artery origin, frequently render endovascular management more
difﬁcult.1,2 New endovascular devices were and continue to be
introduced to address these anatomic difﬁculties, including
stents,3
ﬂow
diverters,4
and
intrasaccular
ﬂow
disruptors
(ISFDs).5
Thus,
although
most
aneurysms
are
increasingly
treated with endovascular methods, many patients with MCA
aneurysms are still managed surgically, but convincing evidence
of which management paradigm is best is lacking.
ISAT (International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial) has shown
that patients with good-grade, small, anterior circulation ruptured
aneurysms (RAs) have better 1-year clinical outcomes after being
coiled,6 a ﬁnding supported by BRAT (Barrow Ruptured Aneurysm
Trial).7 However, there are many reasons to suspect that those
results
do
not
apply
to
aneurysms
located
at
the
MCA
bifurcation. Only 14% of aneurysms in ISAT were on the MCA,
likely because lesions in this location were preferentially treated
with surgery. Even after selection, the MCA subgroup results
were similar for coiling and clipping (relative risk, 1.01 [0.71e
1.45]).8 In the all-inclusive BRAT trial, 21/31 (68%) of MCA an-
eurysms assigned to coiling were crossed over to clipping.9
For unruptured aneurysms, comparative case series suggest that
clipping of MCA aneurysms may lead to better long-term anatomic
outcomes10 but all available data are subject to the shortcomings
of observational studies. The use of novel endovascular devices
such as ﬂow diverters and ISFDs is increasingly reported for
unruptured MCA aneurysms but they have been associated with
substantial perioperative morbidity or suboptimal long-term
angiographic results.4,5
Although patients and clinicians are drawn toward novel endo-
vascular options, surgical clipping is “ﬁghting an uphill battle with
fewer and fewer troops.”11 More than expert opinion and case series
may be required to justify open neurosurgical aneurysm repair in
the future. There are ongoing comparative trials addressing the
best treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) and
RAs in all locations.12,13 These all-inclusive trials do not take into
consideration the speciﬁc nature of MCA aneurysms and their
treatment. Our goal is to present the MCA aneurysm results from
CURES (Collaborative UnRuptured Endovascular vs. Surgery) and
ISAT-2, not because they provide ﬁnal answers to the clinical
dilemma, but because they may serve to inform the conduct of
future trials designed to offer optimal care to patients with MCA
aneurysms.
METHODS
This report was prepared in the spirit of the CONSORT (Consol-
idated Standards of Reporting Trials) recommendations,14 but this
pooled subgroup comparison is not a report of a stand-alone trial.
The study protocols for both the CURES and the ISAT-2 trials
(registered at Clinical trial.gov NCT01139892, NCT01668563) have
been published, and interim results for each trial reported in 2017
and 2019, respectively.12,13,15,16 CURES and ISAT-2 are investigator-
led, pragmatic randomized care trials conducted in North Amer-
ican and European centers. Data capture and management are
through secure servers in compliance with Good Clinical Practice
requirements. Protocols are approved by local institutional review
boards of each participating institution and all patients (or
representatives) provided written informed consent. Case report
forms are simple, and required data collection made sparing to
facilitate completion by normal care personnel. Screening logs are
not required per protocol.
Patients
All patients with MCA aneurysms included in CURES from
inception in September 2010 to July 2020 and all patients with
MCA aneurysms included in ISAT-2 from inception in November
2012 to July 2020 are reported. CURES includes independent
(modiﬁed Rankin Scale [mRS] score <3), adult patients with an
intradural unruptured aneurysm measuring 3e25 mm, with life
expectancy >10 years. ISAT-2 includes any adult patient with an
intradural aneurysm that has ruptured within the last 30 days, for
whom the results of the initial ISAT6 are judged to not necessarily
apply. Patients with grade 5 subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) are
excluded. The patients with MCA aneurysms in CURES and
ISAT-2 were enrolled at 3 Canadian, 1 American, and 3
European participating centers (Montréal, Edmonton, Ottawa,
New York, Lille, Liège, and Barcelona).
Interventions
Patients are treated with surgical clipping or endovascular
techniques as per local practices, with technical details left to
individual operators. The use of stents, ﬂow diverters, and other
novel endovascular devices or adjunctive methods is recorded.
Outcome Measures and Blinding
The primary composite outcome measure of CURES is treatment
failure, deﬁned as 1) failure of intended modality to treat the
aneurysm, 2) intracranial hemorrhage during follow-up, or 3) a
residual aneurysm at 1 year. A crossover from one treatment
modality to the other with no attempt to treat the aneurysm was
not considered to reach the primary outcome of treatment failure.
CURES also records clinical outcomes (mRS score) at discharge

Randomization
For both studies, parallel-group randomization is 1:1, concealed,
and generated by a secure platform (MedSciNet) available 24/7.
Details of minimization algorithms are available.15,16 In May 2016,
the protocols were modiﬁed at 1 center (Centre Hospitalier de
l’Université de Montréal, Montréal) to enable prerandomization.18
Statistical Analyses
Interim subgroup results from ongoing randomized trials are not
usually reported.19 We restricted the use of statistical tests to a
Fisher’s exact test of the primary outcome of patients with MCA

Ten patients with UIA have been enrolled but have not yet been

There were 5/90 (6%) crossovers. Three patients with MCA UIA

management (also patient choice). One surgically managed pa-

The number of surgically managed patients with unruptured

Details regarding treatment-related complications and clinical

None of the patients with surgically managed UIA had the index

There were 5 crossover patients (7%) in ISAT-2: 2 patients with

DISCUSSION
Patients with MCA aneurysms randomly allocated to surgical
clipping had similar clinical outcomes (number of patients with
mRS score >2) but better treatment efﬁcacy outcomes at 1 year
than did patients allocated endovascular treatment. These ﬁndings
require conﬁrmation in a future trial dedicated to patients with
MCA aneurysms.
Our study differs from previous randomized evidence regarding
ruptured MCA aneurysms. First, ISAT included disproportionally
few selected patients with MCA aneurysms (301/2143 or 14%,
compared with 38% in ISAT-2) recruited between 1994 and 2004, a
time when only simple coiling was available. Although ISAT
showed better clinical outcomes for coiled aneurysms at 1 year,
this overall trial result was not conﬁrmed for MCA aneurysms. The
clinical primary end point of mRS score >2 was reached in 39/139
clipped patients (28.1%; 95% CI, 0.21e0.36) and 46/162 coiled
patients
(28.4%; 95% CI, 0.22e0.36).8 However, subgroup
ﬁndings that differ from overall trial results should always be
interpreted with caution.20
The suspicion that only selected patients with MCA aneurysms
were judged eligible for endovascular treatment at the time of
ISAT is supported by the prerandomized BRAT study: of 61 pa-
tients with ruptured MCA aneurysms included between 2003 and
2007, 30 were assigned clipping and 31 coiling. Of the 31 (68%)
endovascular patients, 21 were crossed over to the surgical arm.
Thus, it was reported that “direct comparison of the treatments
was impossible.”9 The Finnish randomized controlled trial (RCT)
on clipping versus coiling21,22 reported only 19 ruptured MCA
aneurysms because 59 patients with MCA aneurysms were
excluded. One RCT reported in 201223 included only 35 patients
with
ruptured
MCA
aneurysms
and
it
did
not
provide
angiographic results per aneurysm location. By contrast, there
were few RA crossovers (7%) in the present study. If most MCA
aneurysms can be treated endovascularly, clinical results of
contemporary technical achievements remain to be properly
compared with surgical clipping. In particular, although overall
clinical results were similar at 1 year in ISAT-2, rebleedings and
retreatments after endovascular treatment are worrisome. This
randomized comparison of angiographic results for ruptured MCA
aneurysms is the largest to date because angiographic follow-ups
were not required in ISAT.

for MCA aneurysms reported complete occlusion rates of 86%
(69/80) for clipped aneurysms versus 27% (20/74) for coiled an-
eurysms at 6e12 months follow-up.27
Meta-analyses of the angiographic results of coiling of MCA
aneurysms focus on “complete or near-complete occlusion” or
“adequate occlusion” rates.28,29 The choice to assimilate complete
occlusions with aneurysm necks comes from RA studies, because
both results have been shown to prevent rebleeding. Complete
occlusions may be more pertinent to the management of UIAs,
because incomplete occlusions and long-term recurrences still
affect many endovascular options while long-term protection from
rupture remains the goal of therapy.
All available evidence on unruptured MCA aneurysms is
observational and all is affected by systemic biases. The present
study is the only randomized comparison of endovascular and
surgical treatment of unruptured MCA aneurysms and results are
compatible with a hypothesis in favor of clipping, because treat-
ment failures were more common after endovascular treatment.
The current study brings the issue of the role of trials in clinical
care to the fore. At the very least, to justify making treatment
decisions in practice, we need to ensure that the alleged increased
efﬁcacy of any more invasive, potentially more risky or novel
option has been proven in a rigorous RCT, a task that remains to
be accomplished for surgical clipping as well as for most recent
endovascular innovations.30-33 Some novel endovascular options
may claim that they can lead to complete aneurysm occlusions
more often than historical endovascular controls, but they have
never been properly compared with the results of surgical
clipping.5
Patients with MCA aneurysms were frequently recruited in both
CURES and ISAT-2, a sign of the ongoing clinical uncertainty
regarding the proper management of these patients. CURES and
ISAT-2 are care trials designed to guide practice in the presence of
uncertainty.34 However, these trials have not been popular with
clinicians and neurovascular centers and recruitment has been
slow, perhaps as a consequence of their pragmatic design and
all-inclusive nature. Patients with MCA aneurysms may need a
speciﬁc trial to ﬁnd out what is the best management of their
condition. For clinicians, a benchmark level of surgical results
obtained in patients with MCA aneurysms whose treatments have
been randomly allocated is needed to establish the merits of
clipping and serve as a reference for which other trials could aim.
The problem is how to determine what constitutes optimal care,
while simultaneously trying to provide optimal care. The solution
is an RCT designed in the best interests of participating patients.35
Clinicians can then offer surgical clipping as optimal care in the
presence of uncertainty through 1:1 randomized allocation of
surgery versus any endovascular option. The additional merit of
such a trial would be that it could help maintain the open
neurosurgical
skills
necessary
for
safe
neurosurgical
management in a ﬁeld now threatened by obsolescence,11 at the
same time offering an opportunity to participate in the progress
of endovascular therapy. The broad outlines of a potential new
trial dedicated to MCA aneurysms is presented in Supplementary
Table 3.
Many surgeons believe clipping to be superior in terms of
long-term angiographic results and it would be tempting to
design the new study accordingly. However, there are multiple
problems with using a surrogate angiographic outcome mea-
sure.36 One systematic difference between the 2 treatments
concerns the use of follow-up imaging studies (CT angiog-
raphy for clipped and MRA for coiled MCA aneurysms). The
diagnostic accuracy of each method in detecting aneurysm
residua differs.37,38 Requiring 1-year catheter angiography for all
patients is unlikely to be clinically acceptable and is contrary to
the pragmatic care trial philosophy.34 Moreover, it is uncertain
whether “same-sized” postcoiling and postclipping residua,
even
on
catheter
angiography,
have
the
same
clinical
signiﬁcance. Our studies suggest more frequent complete
occlusions with clipping. The crucial empirical question, for
clipping as for any endovascular innovation, is how much
more
morbidity
(if
any)
is
justiﬁed
to
achieve
better
angiographic results, and whether angiographic results are
predictive of better long-term outcomes.
Limitations of this study include that it is an unpowered
subgroup analysis of 2 ongoing trials; some patients have yet to be
treated, and not all treated patients have 1-year follow-up results.
The number of patients is small, and the imaging evaluations were
not performed by a core laboratory. The choice to compare
“complete aneurysm occlusion” rates is debatable. Thus the
ﬁndings reported here qualify as exploratory. They should be
interpreted with caution and be used only as hypotheses for future
trials.39 Only a few (10/48, 21%) of the endovascularly managed
cases in this work used adjunctive devices such as stents, ﬂow
diverters, or ISFDs. The hypothesis that more liberal use of
novel endovascular adjuncts could decrease the incidence of
treatment failure or improve angiographic results should be
tested in randomized trials.
We believe that our results should nevertheless encourage
surgeons to participate in a new trial dedicated to aneurysms in
this location. From the endovascular perspective, a variety of
innovative minimally invasive solutions are increasingly tried for
MCA aneurysms. These innovations should also be tested in a
randomized trial.40 Patients recruited in such trials should be
offered at least a chance to be treated surgically. We suggest
that the benchmark to be used to compare endovascular
innovations for MCA aneurysms should be surgical clipping.
CONCLUSIONS
Randomized subgroup data from 2 trials show that better efﬁcacy
may be obtained with surgical management of patients with MCA
aneurysm. This ﬁnding should be conﬁrmed by randomized trials
dedicated to MCA aneurysms.
